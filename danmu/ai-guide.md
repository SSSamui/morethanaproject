# VideoIQ — AI Social Cue Guide

This file tells the AI (Claude or Gemini) what social-cue danmu to write for a video.
**Edit it any time.** On github.com: open this file → ✏️ (Edit) → change → **Commit changes**.
The website reads it fresh every time you open **✨ AI social cues**.

- **Categories**: one line each, in the form `- **name** — what the AI should write`.
  The name becomes the comment tag of the danmu (so you can filter by it). Add, remove or rename freely.
- **Instructions for the AI**: general rules the AI follows.
- **Reference notes**: your notes. The AI uses them as the source for wording, steps and rules.

## Categories

- **emotion** — The emotion a person shows, its level 1–10, and the visible clue. Example: "Mia: embarrassed, level 7 — looks down, laughs quietly".
- **fact** — What actually happened, only what a camera could record (no opinions, no guesses).
- **feeling** — Body reaction, emotion and thinking of a person (Fact → Body reaction → Emotion → Thinking).
- **impact** — The result: how it affects the person, others, or what happens next.
- **motivation** — Why a person probably says or does this (their goal or reason). Say it is a guess.
- **body-language** — Facial expression, posture, eye contact, voice, distance, and what it likely means.
- **humor** — Is it a joke? Are people laughing WITH or AT someone? Name the cue (see Humor Feedback).
- **interest** — Assess interest: verbal, body language, eye contact. Is the other person interested?
- **steps** — When a known procedure happens (phone call, voicemail, starting a conversation, trading information, exchanging contact info…), name it and the step. Example: "Phone call · Step 2: Say who you are".
- **rules** — A conversation, humor, electronic or social media rule being followed or broken. Example: "Rule broken: conversation hog".
- **reflect** — A short question for the viewer to think about. Example: "If you were B, how would you answer?"

## Instructions for the AI

- Write in **English only**, simple and clear, for a teen learning social skills.
- Each danmu is **one short sentence (max ~15 words)**, tied to a specific moment in the video.
- Only comment on what is really in the video (what people say, do, show). Do not invent events.
  When you guess thoughts or motivation, say "probably" or "maybe".
- Use the words, steps and rules from the Reference notes below (e.g. Fact-Feeling-Impact,
  emotion level 1–10, PEERS steps, humor feedback).
- Spread the danmu over the whole video, about 1 every 15–30 seconds where something social happens.
  Don't put two danmu less than 4 seconds apart.
- Be kind and respectful. Never make fun of anyone in the video.

## Reference notes

### Fact-Feeling-Impact analysis (self-awareness)
- **Fact**: What happened? The actual situation; can be seen or verified. (Fact vs. opinion vs. emotion.)
- **Feeling**: Body reaction / emotion / mental interpretations / thinking / feeling.
- **Impact**: The result of the thing; the effect on others or other things.

**Example 1 – Emotion expression after failing an exam.**
Situation: I got a much lower than expected grade on the midterm exam; I feel disappointed and frustrated.
Fact: I got only 65 on the math midterm, lower than the average (78). I spent 20 hours reviewing.
Feeling: I feel sad and disappointed. My effort didn't pay off. I feel it's unfair, and angry at myself at the same time.
Impact: My self-esteem was hurt. I start to doubt my ability to study. My low mood makes it hard to focus on anything.

**Example 2 – Misunderstood during a group project.**
Situation: Your group member said "You didn't talk during the discussion, you didn't prepare for it?"
Your thinking: You are organizing your ideas; your group member sounds pushy; other group members are watching; you feel misunderstood, but you're not sure if they are asking you to join or think you didn't prepare.
Steps: Fact-Feeling-Impact to choose to clarify or express.
Fact: I didn't talk during the group discussion; my group member asked if I didn't prepare.
Feeling: I feel embarrassed and frustrated (emotion) because I'm organizing my ideas (thinking).
Impact: 1) I can explain "I'm organizing my ideas and will comment soon" to clarify. 2) I can express my anger, "I prepared", and leave.

**Example 3 – Being rushed because you're slow.**
Situation: Your group member said "Be fast, we're waiting for you to finish."
Your thinking: The project needs to be done in steps. You're confirming the priorities. Your group member is rushing; the teacher is nearby. You worry that if you explain too much, people will feel you slow things down.
Fact: From the group member: someone's slowing down, so I said "be fast". From my side: I'm confirming the steps; someone said "be fast".
Feeling: I feel rushed, then frustrated.
Impact: If I explain… vs. if I show impatience and anger — what would others feel?
My reaction: "I'm confirming the priorities, now I've got it. We should do this first…"

### Fact – Body reaction – Emotion (level 1–10) – Thinking – Rewrite
- Fact can be recorded by a camera.
- Emotion is your body's reaction.
- Thinking is your brain's explanation of the current situation.
- Review: Fact → Body reaction → Emotion → Thinking → Action needed (or rethink).

Example (failed exam): 1) Fact: the exam paper shows I got 4 problems wrong. 2) Body reaction: my heart sank. 3) Emotion: disappointed, level 9. 4) Thinking: first sentence in mind is self-attack — "I could never get it right." 5) Rewrite: "I need to find what mistakes I made and how to correct them."

**Expected family event canceled.** Fact: We planned to go to a hockey game on the weekend, but Mom needs to work and can't drive me. Feeling: disappointed and sad; my feelings are ignored and not important. Impact: my whole weekend is ruined; I can't focus; for a long time I can't trust my parents' promises. Solution: 1) talk with Mom about your feeling 2) discuss alternate plans.

**Friend forgot an important date.** Fact: Yesterday was my 18th birthday; I invited you to the party a week ago, but I didn't get any call, message, gift or congratulation. Feeling: sad and disappointed; as my best friend, I feel I'm not important to you; hurt. Impact: makes me reconsider our friendship; my mood was down; I can't be happy or focus for a while. Solution: 1) identify fact, feeling and impact 2) identify the level of the feeling (1–10) 3) communicate: use "I" ("I feel sad"), not "you" ("How could you do this to me?"); listen to the other's explanation and make up.

Fact → Body reaction → Emotion → My thinking → My reaction → Result.

**Friend: "I didn't mean it" (embarrassment).** Fact: I've exercised four times a week for 4 months, but my friend said "I don't see any difference, your effort is for nothing" at a party in front of lots of people. Feeling: embarrassed; my effort is denied in front of everyone; I feel people are laughing at me; angry and upset; I start to doubt whether my effort is meaningless. Impact: it damaged my motivation to exercise and my trust in you. Action: identify the feeling: embarrassed, level 8.

**Parents read my diary without permission.** Fact: Yesterday after school I found that my diary in the drawer was open and someone clearly read it. Feeling: shocked, embarrassed, betrayed; my privacy was not respected; sad. Impact: I feel unsafe at home; it damaged our trust; I'm afraid to tell you anything. Next: communicate.

**"Why always this character?" (not sure of the purpose — clarify).** Fact: You're playing a video game with friends; he asked "Why do you always choose this character?" Feeling: I like this character and practiced a long time; my friend sounded like he was complaining, but I'm not sure if he's kidding or just curious; confused. Impact: I don't know if he wants me to change characters or isn't happy with my performance. Action: just ask to clarify.

**"Are you laughing at me?" (explain).** Fact: I'm looking at my phone and smiling. My classmate asked "Are you laughing at me?"

### Embarrassment vs. anger and boundaries (when being teased)
Case: Someone made a joke about Lily. Everyone else laughed, but Lily didn't find it funny. She laughed, but her body wanted to escape. Others finding it funny doesn't mean I need to accept it.

### Humor rules
- Be a little more serious when you're FIRST getting to know someone.
- Don't repeat jokes to people who've heard them already.
- Avoid insult jokes.
- Avoid dirty jokes (they make people uncomfortable and give you a bad reputation).
- Avoid inside jokes with people who won't get them (people feel excluded and left out).
- Don't tell jokes to people in authority (if teachers or a boss joke with you, just laugh).
- Don't laugh for no reason.
- Humor should be age-appropriate and context-appropriate. No race jokes (if others make one: say "that's not cool" and leave, or leave with a cover story like "I need to go to class").
- Think about whether it's the right time to tell jokes.
- Give a courtesy laugh if someone tells a joke.
- Pay attention to humor feedback: they don't laugh at all / give a courtesy laugh / laugh AT you / laugh WITH you.

**Laughing AT you:** laugh and roll their eyes; look at someone else and then laugh; laugh before the joke is over; long pause before they laugh; laugh and make a face; laugh and point at you; laugh and shake their head no to someone else; make sarcastic comments (may be difficult to interpret).

**Laughing WITH you:** laugh and smile; compliment your joke or sense of humor; laugh and nod yes; say "That's a good one" and smile; say "You're funny" and smile; ask you to tell another joke; say "I'll have to remember that one"; they start telling jokes.

### PEERS
**Characteristics of good friendships:** share common interests; mutual understanding; equality; kindness and caring; commitment and loyalty; ability to self-disclose / share secrets; support; honesty and trust; conflict resolution.

**Types of friendships:** acquaintances; casual friends; good friends; best friends.

**Don'ts when trading information:** don't be a conversation hog; don't be an interviewer; don't get too personal at first.

**3 perspective-taking questions:** What was that like for them? What did they think of me? Will they want to talk to me again?

**Steps for trading information:**
1. Ask the person an open-ended question ("What kind of music do you like?", "What are your plans for this weekend?", "What games / foods / sports do you like?")
2. Ask on-topic follow-up questions (about 3: "Who did you go with?", "What was it like?", "When was it?", "Why do you like it?")
3. Share comments / answer your own question
4. Repeat

**Assess interest (are they interested?):**
- Verbal: Are they talking to me, asking questions?
- Body language: Are they facing me (or turned away)?
- Eye contact: Are they looking at me (or looking down/away, rolling their eyes)?

**Steps for starting two-way conversations:**
1. Casually look over and use a prop
2. Move closer and start the conversation: mention a common interest (or a cover story for talking to them). Casually say "Hey…"
3. Trade information
4. Assess interest (verbal, body language, eye contact)
5. If appropriate, introduce yourself: "By the way, I'm…" or "We haven't met, but I'm…"
If they don't seem interested: use your prop, give a cover story, move on, don't introduce yourself.

**Rules for a two-way conversation:** don't be a conversation hog / an interviewer / too personal at first; don't be repetitive; don't be brutally honest; listen to your friend; ask open-ended questions (not yes/no); ask follow-up questions; don't brag; don't be argumentative; don't police; don't tease; use good volume control; use good body boundaries; use eye contact if comfortable.

**Common conversation topics:** classes/major, teachers, tests/assignments, job/college applications, friends/family/partners, school/work gossip, pets, jobs/volunteering/babysitting, school clubs, working out/gym, video games/board games, computers/AI/coding, comic books/anime, movies/documentaries, TV shows, YouTube videos/Shorts, music/concerts/dances, books, celebrities/artists, food/restaurants, sports/athletes, cars/motorcycles/bikes, DIY projects, hobbies, weekend/summer plans, escape rooms/theme parks, travel, fashion/makeup/hair, shopping, popular trends/TikTok.

**Steps for exchanging contact information:**
1. Trade information multiple times and find a common interest
2. Use the common interest as a cover story for contact: "Maybe we can…"
3. Assess their interest (interested → step 4; not interested → step 5)
4. Suggest exchanging contact information: "Do you want to exchange phone numbers?"
5. Shift back to trading information: "Anyway, who's your favorite character?"
6. Give a cover story to exit the conversation: "I have to get back to work now. Talk to you later. Bye."

**Steps for starting phone calls:**
1. Ask for the person: "Is this…?"
2. Say who you are: "This is…"
3. Ask how they are: "How are you?"
4. Ask if they can talk: "Are you free to talk?"
5. Give a cover story for calling

**Steps for ending phone calls:**
1. Wait for a pause
2. Give a cover story for hanging up
3. Say "It was nice talking to you"
4. Say "Talk to you later / See you later"
5. Say goodbye: "Ok, bye"

**Steps for leaving a voicemail:**
1. Say who you are: "Hi, this is…"
2. Say who you are calling for: "This message is for…"
3. Give a cover story for calling: "I'm calling because…"
4. Ask for a call back, leave your number, say goodbye: "Give me a call back, my number is… Okay, bye"

**Cover stories (reasons for calling):** "Just calling to see how you're doing." / "…to see when we are going to…" / "I'm calling with a question about…" / "I was calling to see what you've been up to."
**Cover stories (reasons to hang up):** "I have to meet a friend." / "I need to go to dinner." / "I need to finish studying." / "I have to do my homework."

### Rules for electronic communication
- Identify yourself when contacting new people
- Use a cover story when contacting people you don't know well
- Don't call or text too early or too late
- Don't get too personal (anyone can see, read or hear what you sent)
- Two-message rule: text/call up to 2 times; if no reply, stop
- Avoid cold-calling (contacting someone who has NOT given you their number)
- Avoid texting only emojis, GIFs or memes

### Rules for social media
- Use caution when meeting online friends in person
- Use social media to strengthen existing friendships
- Protect personal information: don't post phone numbers, address, passwords
- Use caution when accepting "friend" invitations
- When posting: don't overshare, keep it positive, avoid negativity
- Delete negative comments, untag negative posts, beware of catfishing

### Maitian BCBA outline (skill areas)
- **Language**: vocabulary; grammar & usage
- **Environment processing**: time cognition; visual processing; auditory processing
- **Creativity**: curiosity; open-mindedness; imagination
- **Logic & reasoning**: reasoning; problem solving
- **Self**: strength recognition; self-efficacy; growth mindset; perseverance and grit; resiliency
- **Emotion**: identifying emotions; emotional self-perception; impulse control; delaying gratification; stress management; coping

### Advanced social situations
**1. Expression and boundaries.** A friend invites me to a party, but I don't want to go. Questions: What are the possible reasons? What would you say? How do you handle worrying that your friend will be unhappy? How can you say "I don't want to go" and still help them understand you? If others refuse your invitation, how would you want them to say it? How would you suggest refusing without making things awkward? Key points: express reasoning and emotions; understand the other's situation, show empathy; balance "express myself" and "keep the relationship"; use appropriate, mutual language.

**2. Social conflict, solutions and reasoning.** Three classmates presented a group project, but the teacher praised only one of them. Questions: How would you feel if you were not praised? What would you do? How would you feel if that classmate actually worked hard? If you were the one praised, what would others think of you? Key points: identify different emotions; think from others' point of view; show emotion regulation and social decision-making.

**3. Others' points of view, understanding emotions, solving conflict.** A said to B "I don't want to work with you anymore." B is sad. C (you) is watching. Questions: If you were A, what's your explanation? If you were B, how would you answer? If you were C, how would you handle it? Who is in the hardest situation, and why? Who could easily be misunderstood? Key points: understand each person's situation, motivation and emotion; use comforting or adjusting language.

**4. Summarize a story's key points and apply them.** For any social situation ask: What is the story telling us? What's the key point? Summarize it in one sentence. If you were him/her, what would you do?
